Scrum in Action: From Theory to Practice

Case Study: Developing a Food-Ordering Application

This fictional case study explores how a restaurant business applies Scrum to develop a mobile food-ordering application. It demonstrates how Scrum accountabilities, events, and artifacts work together to manage uncertainty, respond to challenges, and deliver value.

The Business Challenge

A growing restaurant business is experiencing challenges with its traditional telephone ordering system. During busy periods, employees struggle to manage incoming calls, resulting in longer wait times, incorrect orders, and frustrated customers.

To improve the customer experience and streamline operations, management decides to develop a mobile application that allows customers to browse menus, place orders, make payments, and track deliveries.

However, the business is uncertain which features customers will value most. Developing the entire application before gathering feedback could result in unnecessary costs, wasted effort, and a product that fails to meet customer needs.

Rather than attempting to build everything at once, the organization adopts Scrum to develop the product incrementally, validate assumptions, and adjust priorities based on what it learns.

Building the Scrum Team

A small, cross-functional Scrum Team is established with the skills needed to design, develop, test, and deliver the application.

Product Owner: Setting the Direction

The Product Owner works with restaurant management, employees, and potential customers to understand their needs and establish a Product Goal focused on creating a convenient and reliable digital ordering experience.

The Product Owner is accountable for maximizing product value and ensuring that the most important work is prioritized.

Scrum Master: Supporting Team Effectiveness

The Scrum Master helps the team understand and apply Scrum effectively. They coach the team in self-management, support collaboration, and help address obstacles that could interfere with progress.

Developers: Turning Ideas into a Working Product

The Developers bring together the technical and creative skills needed to design the user experience, build functionality, test features, and maintain quality. They determine how to accomplish the work and are accountable for creating a usable Increment each Sprint.

Creating and Prioritizing the Product Backlog

The Product Owner develops and orders a Product Backlog containing the features and improvements needed to achieve the Product Goal.

Initial Product Backlog items include:

  • Browse restaurant menus and prices
  • View food descriptions and images
  • Create customer accounts
  • Add items to a shopping cart
  • Complete secure online payments
  • Track order status
  • Receive delivery notifications

Rather than treating every feature as equally important, the Product Owner orders the backlog based on customer value, business priorities, and emerging information. Menu browsing becomes an early priority because it provides a foundation for the ordering experience.

Sprint Planning: Turning Priorities into Action

The Scrum Team begins a two-week Sprint by discussing the most valuable work that can be accomplished. Together, they establish a clear Sprint Goal.

Sprint Goal: Enable customers to browse the restaurant's menu and view available food items through a functional mobile interface.

The Developers select Product Backlog items they believe can be completed during the Sprint and develop a plan for delivering them.

The Sprint Goal, selected backlog items, and delivery plan form the Sprint Backlog. This provides a shared focus while allowing the Developers to adjust their approach as new information becomes available.

During the Sprint: Responding to Challenges

Development begins, and the team works collaboratively to create the menu-browsing experience.

During the Daily Scrum, the Developers inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt their plan for the next day.

Midway through the Sprint, the team discovers that high-resolution food images are causing slow loading times on mobile devices. This creates a potential problem for the customer experience.

Instead of waiting until the end of development, the Developers investigate the issue, collaborate on a solution, and optimize the images to improve application performance.

This situation demonstrates the three pillars of Scrum in practice. The problem becomes visible through Transparency, the team evaluates its impact through Inspection, and the Developers adjust their approach through Adaptation.

Delivering a Usable Increment

By the end of the Sprint, the Developers complete a working menu-browsing feature that allows customers to view food items, descriptions, images, and prices.

The functionality has been tested and meets the team's Definition of Done, meaning it satisfies the required quality standards and forms part of a usable product Increment.

Although the application does not yet support payments or delivery tracking, the team has created a foundation for future development.

Sprint Review: Learning from Stakeholders

At the Sprint Review, the Scrum Team and stakeholders inspect the completed functionality and discuss progress toward the Product Goal.

Restaurant representatives explore the menu feature and suggest adding dietary filters so customers can easily identify vegetarian, vegan, and gluten-free options.

This feedback reveals an opportunity to improve the product that was not originally considered a priority.

The Product Owner considers the new information and adapts the Product Backlog. The team can now make more informed decisions about which features to develop next.

Sprint Retrospective: Improving Team Effectiveness

Following the Sprint Review, the Scrum Team holds a Sprint Retrospective to reflect on how effectively it worked during the Sprint.

Although the team successfully delivered the menu feature, members recognize that performance testing happened later than expected.

The team discusses what contributed to the problem and agrees to introduce performance checks earlier in future Sprints.

This improvement helps strengthen development practices and reduces the likelihood of similar problems occurring again.

The Outcome: Delivering Value Through Learning

At the end of the Sprint, the restaurant has a usable menu-browsing feature rather than simply a detailed plan for a future application.

More importantly, the organization has gained valuable information about customer preferences, identified technical challenges, and improved its approach to product development.

Future Sprints can build on this foundation by introducing shopping cart functionality, online payments, and order tracking while continuing to incorporate stakeholder feedback.

The case study demonstrates how Scrum supports incremental delivery and continuous learning. Rather than treating the original plan as fixed, the team uses evidence and feedback to guide decisions throughout development.

Key Takeaways

  • Scrum accountabilities establish clear responsibilities while supporting shared ownership of outcomes.
  • Scrum events create regular opportunities to plan, inspect progress, gather feedback, and improve.
  • Scrum artifacts and their commitments make work, goals, and quality expectations visible.
  • Transparency, inspection, and adaptation help teams respond to uncertainty and make informed decisions.
  • Incremental delivery allows organizations to learn from usable product features rather than relying entirely on assumptions.

The Bottom Line: Scrum is more than a collection of meetings, responsibilities, and processes. It is a framework that connects people, goals, and feedback to support informed decisions, continuous improvement, and meaningful product delivery.

Note: This is a fictional educational case study created to demonstrate the practical application of the Scrum framework.
